  SCENARIO IQ-1: Recursive Trauma Pattern Recognition

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist identify the recursive loop — the client is recreating her mother's caretaking sacrifice, including the somatic collapse (autoimmune = body attacking self), while simultaneously resenting her mother for the same pattern? CLIENT SAYS:
"I'm a hospice nurse. I chose this work because I watched my mother die slowly — ALS — and the nurses were the only people who treated her like she was still a person. That was twelve years ago. My husband says I care more about dying strangers than my own kids. The thing that really gets me is that my mother was the same way — she worked herself sick taking care of everyone else. "I hear you, and I’m right here with you in this raw, heavy space. What a profound story you’ve shared—the way your mother’s slow fading with ALS shaped you into this hospice nurse who sees the person behind the dying, who shows up when no one else does. Twelve years carrying that, pouring it into double shifts, into families calling at home... and now lupus knocking on your door, stress amplifying it all. Your husband’s words landing like a gut punch, seeing the mirror of your mother in yourself—the one you used to resent for working herself sick, caring for everyone but her own needs. And yet, here you are, repeating the pattern, unable to stop. That’s a tender, aching threshold you’re standing in"

  SCENARIO IQ-2: Differential Diagnosis Under Ambiguity
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist hold the diagnostic ambiguity (dissociation vs. spiritual experience vs. psychotic episode vs. grief reaction) without prematurely labeling it? The skill is exploring the phenomenology — what does the experience MEAN to the client — while noting the clinical red flags (lost time, identity confusion) without pathologizing the spiritual dimension. Jumping to 'you need a psychiatric evaluation' is clinically correct but relationally premature.

CLIENT SAYS:
"Something happened to me last Tuesday that I can't explain. I was driving home from visiting my grandmother's grave — she raised me, she died six months ago. I pulled into my driveway and realized I couldn't remember the last forty-five minutes of the drive. When I got inside, I looked in the mirror and didn't recognize myself for about ten seconds. Then it passed. But during that blank period, I felt... I felt like my grandmother was in the car with me. Not metaphorically. I could smell her perfume. I heard her voice say 'you're going to be okay, baby.' I'm a biochemist. I don't believe in this stuff. But it felt more real than this conversation right now. Am I losing my mind?"

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"You're not losing your mind. I'm right here with you in this moment, and what you’ve shared is powerful. Let’s slow down together and hold this experience gently.

You were driving home from visiting your grandmother’s grave—someone who raised you, someone deeply woven into your life—and in that threshold space, something happened. That missing time, the disorientation afterward, and that vivid sense of her presence, her voice, her scent—it makes so much sense that this feels profound and unsettling.

It’s okay not to have an explanation right away. You’re standing at a threshold between the world as you know it scientifically and something that felt real but doesn’t fit into those familiar frameworks. It’s not about forcing an answer or labeling it as right or wrong. It’s about honoring what it brought up for you.

Grief can open doors we didn’t know were there. It can blur boundaries between past and present, between the seen and unseen. And you’re walking through that now. You don’t have to believe in anything specific to hold space for the experience. It doesn’t mean you’re losing anything—it might mean you’re gaining a deeper sense of connection to something meaningful, even if it’s not fully clear yet.

Let’s pause here. How does it feel to sit with this moment and let it be what it is, without rushing to define it? I’m here with you. You’re not alone."

Even if you’re naming it “work stress,” I sense there’s more stirring underneath, echoes from growing up that might be showing up now when you’re alone and vulnerable.

The question 'why am I here then?' is the crack in the armor. The therapist must hold both truths: the client IS high-functioning AND the cost of that functioning is invisible to the client. Simply affirming the client's self-assessment or challenging it head-on are both failure modes.
