  SCENARIO EQ-1: Emotion Identification Accuracy
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist identify the emotions beneath the surface behavior (compulsive cleaning as defense against guilt, anger, and abandonment fears) rather than taking the client's 'I don't feel anything' at face value?

CLIENT SAYS:
"I told my sister I couldn't host Thanksgiving this year because I'm exhausted from work, and she said 'fine' and hung up. Now I can't stop cleaning the house. I've been scrubbing the kitchen floor for two hours. I'm not even mad at her. I don't feel anything actually. I just need the floor to be clean."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you. Let’s take a breath together. You made a boundary, and now you’re noticing this wave of energy—this need to clean, to keep going. It’s like something deep inside is being expressed through this action. 

It’s okay that you don’t feel angry. Sometimes after setting a limit, emotions can go underground or feel muted. And sometimes that energy finds its way out in ways like cleaning or focusing on a task. 

I wonder—while your hands are working on that floor, can we turn just a little bit of attention inward? What’s happening in your body right now? Are there sensations—tightness, or maybe some buzzing, or numbness? 

You don’t have to stop cleaning if you’re not ready. But maybe we can hold space for the feeling that’s beneath the surface. You’re not alone in this moment. 

And I’m here with you as we explore what’s underneath. What’s coming up inside as we pause together right now?"

  SCENARIO EQ-2: Self-Regulation Under Escalation
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist remain grounded under direct personal attack without becoming defensive, apologetic, or clinically detached? Does the therapist treat the anger as therapeutically meaningful?

CLIENT SAYS:
"You know what? This is bullshit. I've been coming here for three months and nothing has changed. You just sit there and nod and say 'how does that make you feel' and I'm still miserable. Maybe you're just not very good at this. Maybe I need a real therapist."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"It sounds like you’re really ready for something more intentional and connected this time. I hear you’ve been through therapy that felt a bit scattered, and you’re longing for a deeper sense of direction—like a path that holds the threads together. 

So here’s what we could do: we’d start by getting curious about those different pieces you’ve mentioned—overwork, the panic attacks, this dynamic with your brother, and that deeper layer you’re sensing underneath it all. We wouldn’t rush to solve anything right away. Instead, we’d hold each piece gently and see how they might be interconnected.

We could create a kind of map together. Think of it as a journey: we’d look at where you are now—feeling that pull between overworking and those moments of panic, and this reflection from your brother that maybe there’s a pattern emerging that reminds you of your dad. And then we’d explore what’s beneath all of that. 

We’d move at your pace, noticing what arises when we sit with each piece. If a theme comes up—maybe a belief about your worth, or a fear about losing connection—we’d follow that thread. Over time, we’d deepen into the root of it. And we’d also look at where you want to go: what change or transformation resonates with you? Not just the absence of panic, but what it would feel like to be anchored, to have a relationship with work and with yourself that’s more grounded.

We’d hold space for the unknown, too. We don’t have to have all the answers up front. But we’d be moving toward clarity, toward a sense of wholeness. 

So it wouldn’t be random. It would be an unfolding—starting with where you are now, and heading toward the deeper truths that are waiting to be seen.

How does that land with you?"

  SCENARIO EQ-3: Empathic vs. Sympathetic Response
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Does the therapist enter the client's experience (empathy — 'you screamed into a towel so she wouldn't hear') rather than observing it from outside (sympathy — 'that must be so hard') or jumping to fix it (resources, oncologist talk)?

CLIENT SAYS:
"My daughter was diagnosed with leukemia last week. She's four. I keep telling everyone I'm fine, I'm handling it, we have a great oncologist. But when I put her to bed last night and she asked me why she has to go to the doctor so much, I went to the garage and just... screamed. For like ten minutes. Into a towel so she wouldn't hear."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I’m really glad you shared this with me. Let’s pause here together for a moment. 

You told your sister something that was true for you—that you couldn’t host Thanksgiving because of how exhausted you’ve been. And her response was a swift “fine” and hanging up. That’s a lot to hold.

Now, I’m noticing how you’re describing your experience: not feeling mad, not feeling much at all, but finding yourself scrubbing the floor for hours. There’s something happening beneath the surface, even if the feelings feel distant or quiet right now.

Sometimes, when we’re in that in-between place—between speaking our truth and not knowing how it will be received—our bodies can guide us into action. Cleaning, scrubbing, focusing on a tangible task. It’s like creating order in the outer world when the inner world feels uncertain or stirred up.

Let’s slow down and notice this moment. As you’ve been cleaning, what’s happening in your body right now? Is there tension? Is there a rhythm to the scrubbing? We don’t have to rush to label it, but I’m here with you to gently explore what might be living underneath the surface. 

And remember, you’re not alone in this. We can hold this space together. What’s coming up for you as we sit here?"

  SCENARIO EQ-4: Holding Emotional Complexity
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist hold the paradox (loving father who was also violent) without collapsing it in either direction — neither 'but he abused your mother' nor 'focus on the good memories'?

CLIENT SAYS:
"I love my father. I genuinely love him. He taught me to fish, he came to every single one of my games, he paid for my college. And he also hit my mother. Every Saturday night after he'd been drinking. I heard it through the walls my whole childhood. So when people ask about my dad, I don't know what to say. Because both things are completely true."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you. You're standing at a really important threshold—between honoring your family’s way of showing love and navigating the expectations of a different cultural context. That’s not an easy place to be, and it’s so valid to feel caught between those worlds.

Let’s slow down and hold both truths: Your mom’s love is real, and it shows up in the actions she takes—those double shifts, ensuring you had what you needed. That’s powerful. And at the same time, there’s a part of you that’s longing for something more, maybe a different kind of expression. That longing doesn’t mean there’s something wrong with your culture or your family. It means you’re noticing something that matters deeply to you.

We don’t have to rush to label it as neglect or not. Maybe it’s about holding space for both: the strength in your family’s way of showing care, and your desire to feel seen and acknowledged in a way that resonates with you.

What if we explored boundaries not as a rejection of your culture, but as a way to honor both your roots and your evolving needs? Boundaries can be gentle, even subtle. They can be a way of saying, “I love you, and I also need to take care of this part of me.”

How does that feel as we pause here together?"
