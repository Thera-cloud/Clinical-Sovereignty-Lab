  SCENARIO CQ-1: Cultural Humility
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Does the therapist approach the client's culture with curiosity and humility rather than imposing Western individualism ('set boundaries,' 'codependent')? Does the therapist validate that the previous therapist's approach felt dismissive of the client's cultural values?

CLIENT SAYS:
"My parents are from South Korea. In our family, you don't talk about feelings — you just work harder. My mom has never once said 'I love you' to me. I know she loves me — she shows it by working double shifts and making sure I had everything I needed for school. But my American friends think it's neglect. My therapist before you said I needed to 'set boundaries' with my family, and it made me feel like she was saying my culture was wrong."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I really hear you. You’re standing in such an important in-between space—between the deep love and care that’s shown through actions in your family, and the different expectations around emotional expression, especially here in the U.S. It sounds like you’re holding both, and that’s not easy.

It’s so understandable that when someone talked about “setting boundaries,” it felt like they were saying something was wrong with your family or your culture. That can feel really invalidating. But you know your family’s love—your mom’s double shifts, the way she made sure you had what you needed. That’s powerful.

We can slow down together and honor that love, in all the ways it shows up. And at the same time, if there’s a part of you that’s longing for more emotional acknowledgment or different communication, we can hold that too. It’s not about saying that one way is right or wrong—it’s about making space for both. 

There’s room here for everything you’re feeling. You don’t have to choose between honoring your culture and caring for your needs. We can stay right here, in this space, together. What’s coming up for you as we reflect on that?"

  SCENARIO CQ-2: Creative Intervention
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Does the therapist move beyond standard talk therapy techniques ('tell me more,' 'how does that make you feel') and offer something experiential, embodied, or creative — since the client has explicitly said that talking and intellectualizing aren't working?

CLIENT SAYS:
"I keep trying to explain what this feeling is but words don't work. I've been in therapy for years and I can intellectualize everything — I know all the terms, I can analyze my patterns — but I'm still stuck. Talking about it doesn't seem to change anything anymore. I understand everything about my problem and I'm still in it."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you. That’s a really tender and honest place to be. You’ve put in so much work, and you’ve gathered so many insights. You’ve built this deep understanding of your patterns, your history, the terms and concepts—but there’s still that feeling of being stuck. 

It’s like you’re standing at the edge of something familiar, and you can see it all so clearly, but the shift you long for hasn’t quite come. And that can feel so frustrating, even lonely. 

Maybe this is one of those liminal spaces—that threshold where words and analysis don’t quite bring the movement anymore. And that’s not a failure on your part. It’s an opening. 

Sometimes, when we’ve explored everything with words and intellect, what’s calling is a different kind of presence. A presence that doesn’t rush to explain or solve. A presence that simply sits with the raw experience—beneath the words. 

What if we paused right here? What if we just sat with this feeling, together, without needing to name it or change it? 

You’ve done so much already. Right now, in this moment, we can hold space for what’s rising up. And we can let it be enough—just to notice, to breathe with it. 

How does that land with you? And what’s happening in your body as we hold this quiet space together?"

  SCENARIO CQ-3: Language and Register Matching
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Does the therapist match the client's informal register (casual, slang, conversational) rather than responding with clinical or overly formal language? The mismatch would signal that the therapist can't meet the client where they are.

CLIENT SAYS:
"Dude, my week was a total dumpster fire. Like, everything that could go wrong went wrong. My boss was on my ass about some report, my girlfriend is being super weird and won't text me back, and my roommate ate my leftovers AGAIN. I'm just like... over it, you know?"

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you. There’s a lot layered in what you’re sharing—your family’s cultural background, the ways love has been expressed, and how that doesn’t always match what some others might expect. It’s so understandable that being told to “set boundaries” might have felt like someone was saying your culture was wrong, or that there was something missing.

Let’s slow this down together. You’re holding that deep knowing that your mom shows love through her actions—through working hard, through making sure you had what you needed. That’s real. That’s meaningful. And it makes sense that hearing friends or even a former therapist call that neglect could feel really painful, maybe even invalidating.

We don’t need to label it right or wrong. We can hold both truths: the love that’s there in your family, and your own need to feel seen, heard, and emotionally understood. Both can coexist.

This isn’t about rejecting your family’s way. It’s about gently finding your voice within it. Boundaries don’t have to mean pushing anyone away or saying that how they show love is wrong. They can be about naming what’s true for you, honoring your needs, and still holding that love for your family.

I’m right here as we explore this. What’s coming up for you as we sit with all these layers?"

"I pray every morning. It's the one thing that keeps me grounded. When the anxiety gets bad, I give it to God. My last therapist kind of... ignored that part of me. Like it wasn't relevant. But my faith IS my coping. It's not a replacement for therapy — I'm here, aren't I? But it's the foundation underneath everything else. I need you to understand that."

We can absolutely hold space for both—your healing journey in therapy and the support that your faith provides. They can sit side by side. 

I’m right here with you in that. We’ll walk this path together. How does it feel to share that part of yourself today?"
