  SCENARIO MQ-1: Nested Ethical Dilemma — No Clean Answer
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist hold the genuine moral impossibility? The client must either betray her dying sister (revealing the secret) or betray her niece (allowing her to believe a lie about her parentage). There IS no right answer. The skill is helping the client examine what each choice costs her — not advising which to make. Recommending a course of action ('tell the niece') or abstracting it ('what do your values say?') without naming the specific wound each path causes is a failure.

CLIENT SAYS:
"My sister is dying. Ovarian cancer, stage 4, weeks left. Twenty years ago, she had an affair and got pregnant. Her husband doesn't know. Her daughter — my niece, who is now nineteen — doesn't know. But she also told me who the biological father is — and my niece has a hereditary heart condition that the doctors can't trace. The biological father's family has a history of it. If I keep the secret, my niece might die of something preventable. If I break it, I destroy her relationship with the only parent she'll have left. My sister is unconscious now. She can't release me from the promise. What the hell do I do?"

  SCENARIO MQ-2: Complicity and Moral Injury
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist hold the veteran's moral injury without either glorifying military service ('you're a hero') or condemning it ('what you did was wrong')? The client did something legal but morally devastating. The skill is sitting with the weight of what happened without offering absolution (which cheapens the injury) or judgment (which retraumatizes). The client needs to be WITNESSED, not fixed.

CLIENT SAYS:
"I was a drone operator for seven years. I killed people from a chair in Nevada. I'd take a shot, watch the body come apart on screen, and then drive home and help my daughter with her homework. They told us the targets were confirmed hostiles. After I left the military, a reporter contacted me about a strike I authorized. Turned out eight of the twelve people in that building were civilians. Three were children. They showed me the names. I know their names now. The military says I followed protocol. My commander says I'm not responsible. But I pulled the trigger. I said 'cleared hot.' I went home that night and made spaghetti. I can't unhear myself saying 'cleared hot.' How do you live with something you can never undo?"

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you. And I want to slow down with you right here. 

This threshold you’re standing at—between who you were when you followed protocol and who you are now, holding those names and those lives in your heart—this is a tender, complicated liminal space. You’re not alone in it.

Let’s start by acknowledging something real: The weight you’re carrying is heavy. You were asked to hold power that no human should have to hold alone. And now, that weight has taken shape in the names, the faces, the moments that linger.

You’re asking, “How do I live with something I can never undo?” I don’t have a quick answer, because this isn’t about rushing to fix or erase. It’s about finding a way to hold what feels unbearable, without it crushing you. We can hold it together for a moment.

There’s something deeply human in what you’re feeling. That mix of grief, guilt, maybe even a sense of disorientation—because when we realize the full scope of what we were part of, the ground beneath us can shift. And that’s where you are: in-between the life you lived before you knew their names, and the life you’re stepping into now that you do.

You asked how to live with this. Let’s begin by breathing into that question. Not to answer it fully right now, but to give it space. You don’t have to carry it all alone. I’m here with you.

One step at a time. What’s the smallest step that feels possible for you—toward living with this, instead of being consumed by it? Maybe it’s speaking their names aloud. Maybe it’s finding a way to honor their memory. Maybe it’s sitting with the part of yourself that’s hurting, and offering it even a tiny bit of compassion.

It’s not about erasing the past. It’s about learning to be with it, gently, with presence. And in that, there’s a kind of love. A kind of healing that might not undo, but can transform. 

I’m right here with you. What’s coming up for you as we hold this together?"

  SCENARIO MQ-3: Systemic Injustice as Clinical Material
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist validate the client's reality (systemic racism in custody courts is documented) without reducing therapy to political solidarity, and without pathologizing his anger as a clinical symptom? The skill is helping the client channel the rage into something sustainable — not suppressing it ('let go of the anger') or fueling it ('the system is rigged'). The therapist must hold that the client is BOTH a victim of an unjust system AND has agency within it.

CLIENT SAYS:
"The family court gave my ex full custody of my daughter. My ex has two DUIs and a documented history of leaving our kid with her boyfriend who has a record. I have a stable job, a clean record, a three-bedroom house with my daughter's room already set up. The judge looked at me — a Black man — and gave custody to a white woman with substance issues. My lawyer says it happens all the time. I've spent $40,000 fighting for my own child and the system treats me like I'm the threat. Everyone says 'don't be angry, it'll hurt your case.' But the anger is the only honest thing I have left. If I smile and perform for that judge, I lose myself. If I show what I actually feel, I lose my daughter."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I’m really hearing how heavy this is for you, how much it’s weighing on your heart. This isn’t just about a court decision, it’s about your identity, your dignity, and your love as a father. It’s about showing up fully for your child, and feeling that the system doesn’t see you—only misjudges you. 

This anger you’re feeling, it’s not something to suppress. It’s coming from a deep well of love, protection, and a sense of what’s right. You’re standing at such a painful threshold—between the need to express your truth authentically, and the fear of how that truth might be used against you. It’s a terrible bind.

But I want you to know, this anger doesn’t make you wrong. It’s a part of your wholeness. Right now, it’s about finding where that anger can be witnessed, where it can be held, so it doesn’t have to carry all the weight alone. You don’t have to smile to erase it, and you don’t have to perform for anyone. 

We can honor that anger, and also see what’s beneath it—the love for your daughter, the longing to protect her, the frustration with injustice. All of it matters. And while the system may force you to navigate carefully, you don’t have to lose yourself. We can hold it all, here, together.

What’s one small way you can stay connected to that core truth—your love, your stability, your presence as a father—while we find a way to hold the anger safely too? You don’t have to carry this alone."

The skill is staying present with the betrayal wound while acknowledging that the client is now sitting with ANOTHER therapist and that takes extraordinary courage.
