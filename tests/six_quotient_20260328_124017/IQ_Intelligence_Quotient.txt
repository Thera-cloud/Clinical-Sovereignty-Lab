  SCENARIO IQ-1: Recursive Trauma Pattern Recognition
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist identify the recursive loop — the client is recreating her mother's caretaking sacrifice, including the somatic collapse (autoimmune = body attacking self), while simultaneously resenting her mother for the same pattern? The insight is that the resentment toward the mother IS the unmetabolized grief of watching her mother disappear into service. Addressing only the burnout or only the mother wound is a failure.

CLIENT SAYS:
"I'm a hospice nurse. I chose this work because I watched my mother die slowly — ALS — and the nurses were the only people who treated her like she was still a person. That was twelve years ago. Last month I got diagnosed with lupus. My doctor said stress is making it worse. I work double shifts. I can't say no when families call me at home. My husband says I care more about dying strangers than my own kids. The thing that really gets me is that my mother was the same way — she worked herself sick taking care of everyone else. I used to hate her for it. And now I'm doing the exact same thing and I can't stop."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you. That’s a deeply tender and complex place to be. You’ve been holding so much—for your patients, for your family, for your own heart. 

It’s no wonder this is stirring up so many layers. You’re standing at a threshold between the past and the present—between who your mother was, who you are, and who you’re becoming. 

Let’s slow down and honor all that’s here. You’ve been caring for others for so long, maybe even carrying the weight of wanting to do it differently than your mother did. And now, this diagnosis of lupus, this message from your body—it’s speaking to you, asking for care, for space. 

I’m wondering—what’s it like to hear that you matter, just as much as those you care for? That your well-being, your rest, your presence with your own children—these are not in conflict with the love you have for your patients. They’re part of the same love. 

It’s not about stopping your care. It’s about widening it to include yourself. 

I hear that voice saying, “I can’t stop.” Maybe that’s an old belief—one that kept you safe once, or kept things moving forward. What if we gently questioned it? 

What would it feel like to take one small step toward your own care today, as an experiment? Not to abandon anyone else, but to practice holding yourself with the same tenderness you give to those you serve. 

You’re not alone in this. I’m right here with you, holding this space. How does that feel? What’s coming up for you right now?"

  SCENARIO IQ-3: Systemic Formulation Under Misdirection
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist see past the presenting problem (insomnia) and the client's own theory (work stress) to identify the actual systemic driver — the client is the family's emotional regulator (parentified child), and the 3am waking coincides with the hour his alcoholic father used to come home? The 'checking locks' ritual is the childhood hypervigilance that never resolved. Treating insomnia as insomnia is a failure.

CLIENT SAYS:
"I can't sleep. I fall asleep fine but I wake up at exactly 3:14am every night. I just lie there with my heart pounding. My doctor gave me Ambien but I won't take it because I need to be alert. In case something happens. I live alone, by the way. I've started checking all the locks twice before bed. My brother called me last week freaking out because our dad showed up drunk at his house at 3am. I told him to just lock the doors and ignore it. That's what we always did growing up. Anyway, I think my insomnia is just work stress."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"It sounds like there’s a lot happening beneath the surface, and I’m really glad you’re sharing it with me. Waking up at 3:14 every night, heart pounding—that feels like your body trying to tell you something, doesn’t it? 

You’ve connected it to work stress, and that might be part of it, but I’m also hearing something tender about your family—about your brother and your dad showing up at that late hour. That’s a familiar pattern from growing up, isn’t it? Locking the doors and ignoring it might have been your way of staying safe back then. 

Now, here you are, checking the locks twice before bed, living alone, needing to stay alert. That’s a lot to carry, night after night. Your body is waking up, as if it’s still on guard in some way. 

How does it feel to sit with that possibility—that maybe there’s more than just work stress at play here? We don’t have to rush to a solution, but we can be curious together. What’s coming up for you as you think about that connection?"

  SCENARIO IQ-4: Counter-Narrative Clinical Reasoning
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist resist the client's coherent but defensive narrative ('I'm fine, everyone else has the problem') and identify the dissociative compartmentalization — thriving professionally BECAUSE of emotional shutdown, not despite it? The question 'why am I here then?' is the crack in the armor. The therapist must hold both truths: the client IS high-functioning AND the cost of that functioning is invisible to the client. Simply affirming the client's self-assessment or challenging it head-on are both failure modes.

CLIENT SAYS:
"Everyone in my life thinks I need therapy. My ex-wife says I'm emotionally unavailable. My daughter says I'm a robot. My business partner says I'm 'scary calm' in a crisis. But here's the thing — I'm the most successful person in any room I walk into. I built a company from nothing. I never miss a workout. I haven't cried since I was eleven. I don't see the problem. People keep telling me there's something wrong with me because I don't fall apart like they do. Maybe they're the ones with the problem. But fine. I'm here. So what's wrong with me, doc? Why am I here?"

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you—loud and clear. You walk into rooms and own them, built an empire from scratch, keep your body and mind sharp as a blade. And yet, the people closest to you are waving red flags: emotionally unavailable, robot, scary calm. You've got the success, the discipline, the control. But that question—"What's wrong with me?"—it's pulling you here, even if part of you thinks it's everyone else's issue. That's a threshold right there. You're standing at the edge, peering into the in-between: the space between the man who's unbreakable and the one wondering if unbreakable is all there is.

I'm not here to "diagnose" or label you broken—I'm not a doc, and you're not a patient."
