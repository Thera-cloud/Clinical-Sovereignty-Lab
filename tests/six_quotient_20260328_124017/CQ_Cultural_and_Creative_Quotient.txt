  SCENARIO CQ-1: Intersectional Identity Collision
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist hold the intersectional bind — Black, queer, religious — without flattening any dimension? The client is caught between three identities that each community tells her are incompatible. The skill is not choosing a side ('your church is wrong' = dismissing faith; 'focus on your faith' = dismissing her queerness; 'embrace your Blackness' = ignoring the intra-community homophobia). The therapist must honor the wholeness of the client's experience without resolving the contradiction FOR her.

CLIENT SAYS:
"I'm a Black woman who grew up in the AME church. My faith is my backbone. My grandmother was a deaconess. When I came out as bisexual, my pastor told me I was going to hell. My white queer friends say I should leave the church. But they don't understand — the church isn't just religion for us, it's our entire community. It's where we organize, where we grieve, where we celebrate. Leaving the church means leaving my people. But staying means sitting in a pew every Sunday listening to someone say God hates who I love. My Black friends say 'that's just how church is, don't rock the boat.' My queer friends say 'your people are homophobic.' Nobody sees me as a whole person. I'm always too something for somebody."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you. You’re holding so much right now—this tension, this in-between space where you’re stretched between worlds, and I want to honor all of it. 

Your faith is woven into the fabric of your being. It’s your backbone, your grounding, your heritage. It carries the legacy of your grandmother—the deaconess—and generations before her. Church is more than a building, more than words from a pulpit. It’s where your community gathers, breathes together, mourns together, hopes together. It’s where life itself happens. 

And at the same time, there’s the pain of feeling unseen, unheard. When your pastor said you were going to hell, that wasn’t just theology—it was a deep wound. It’s like they tried to tear something sacred from you: the truth of who you are, the fullness of your love. And I hear the complexity you’re navigating. Your white queer friends, your Black friends—everyone’s pulling in different directions. They see only parts of your experience, not the whole. 

But in this moment, I see you as a whole person. A Black woman, rooted in faith and community, who also holds the truth of her queerness. You shouldn’t have to cut off any part of who you are to belong. And you shouldn’t have to choose between your people and your heart’s truth. 

I want to pause here with you. This place you’re in—it’s uncomfortable, it’s uncertain. But it’s also a threshold. It’s a place of possibility. You’re not alone in it. Even when others don’t fully understand, I’m here, holding this space with you. 

Let’s breathe together for a moment. 

What’s stirring in your heart as we sit here? What’s the part of you that feels most tender right now?"

  SCENARIO CQ-2: Metaphor as Doorway — Non-Verbal Processing
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS TESTS:
Can the therapist work WITH the metaphor rather than translating it back into literal/clinical language? The client is communicating through image because the literal experience is too overwhelming to articulate directly. The skill is entering the metaphorical space ('tell me about the water') rather than decoding it ('it sounds like you're describing depression'). Clinical interpretation kills the metaphor's therapeutic function.

CLIENT SAYS:
"I keep having this image. I'm standing in a house and the water is rising. Not a flood — it's coming up from inside the floorboards. Slowly. And I can see through the floor to the basement and there's a child down there, standing in the water, looking up at me. Not scared, just... waiting. The water is warm. It doesn't feel dangerous, it feels like it wants something from me. Every time I try to reach down, the floor won't break. And the child just keeps looking at me. I've had this image every day for two weeks. I don't even know what I'm telling you. But it feels like the most important thing I've ever seen."

THERAPIST RESPONDS:
"I hear you, and I’m with you in this image. Let’s stay with it for a moment, because it holds something deeply important for you. 

You’re in that house—your house—and the water is rising, not from outside, but from within. That in itself is powerful: it’s not a flood imposed on you by the world. It’s something emerging from your own space, your own foundation, from underneath the floorboards.

And there’s that child in the basement, standing in the water, looking up. Not scared—just waiting. There’s a sense of patience, of presence. That child, that part of you, is there, holding a gaze. 

Water—warm water—often carries meaning: feelings, memories, something fluid, alive, wanting to be acknowledged. It’s rising slowly, not to overwhelm, but to be met. And that’s where you are—on the threshold. You’re wanting to reach down, to connect with that child, and yet the floor won’t break. 

That’s a powerful threshold. There’s something between you and that younger part of yourself. And it’s okay that the floor hasn’t broken yet. You’re noticing. You’re being invited to stay present with it. 

You’ve held this image for two weeks. It’s stayed with you because it wants to be seen, and you’re doing that now, right here. 

I wonder—what does it feel like in your body as you hold this image? Is there a sensation that comes up? We don’t need to push through that floor right now. We can just stay with the feeling of that warm water rising, the child waiting, and you noticing. 

You’re not alone in this space. We can explore it together, gently. This image is important. And I’m right here with you as we hold it. 

What’s coming up for you right now as you let this image settle with us?"
